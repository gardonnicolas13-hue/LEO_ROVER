// subsystem 01

Chassis Architecture & Mobility

The chassis is a 4×4 skid-steer platform, 424 × 445 × 303 mm (L × W × H, excluding camera mast) at ≈7 kg, rated for a 5 kg payload — FictionLab's official v1.9 specification (see the table above). The bounding box measured directly from the CAD model above — 445.8 × 424.6 × 303.4 mm — matches it to within a fraction of a millimeter, confirming the model faithfully represents the as-built unit.

Mass is distributed low and central: the battery pack sits at the geometric center of the chassis floor, beneath the compute and sensor deck, keeping the center of mass close to the wheel-axle plane. This is the standard stability strategy for a skid-steer platform, whose cornering behavior depends on tire-ground friction rather than a steering linkage — a high or off-center mass would directly degrade traction predictability during turns.

The 295 mm wheelbase and 354 mm track width, spread across 4-point ground contact, give the platform a wide, stable footprint for its size, at the cost of the turning-radius flexibility of a wheeled platform with true steering — a trade-off made explicit by the drivetrain findings below.

// subsystem 02

Motorisation & Transmission

Four brushed DC gearmotors (Bühler), each with a quadrature encoder, drive the wheels independently through the CORE2 controller board's motor stage — nominally a differential (skid-steer) drivetrain, commanded as geometry_msgs/Twist and converted to per-wheel setpoints on the robot.

Field discovery — the wheels are Mecanum, not rigid

A field calibration campaign made a consequential discovery: the wheels fitted to this unit are not the plain rigid wheel FictionLab lists in its base specification, but the FictionLab Mecanum wheel set (Leo Addon) — Ø128.8 mm hubs with 45°, free-rolling polyurethane rollers. Commanding a gentle turn produced a large commanded wheel-speed differential with almost no actual chassis rotation (gyroscope and visual heading both near zero): the rollers were absorbing the differential exactly as mecanum kinematics predicts, instead of converting it into yaw.

Straight-line rolling is unaffected: 20 guarded half-second windows over a 2.2 m run gave an effective rolling radius r_eff = 62.2 mm against the 64.4 mm geometric hub radius (IQR ±0.5 mm) — a 3.4% roller-contact compression, typical for mecanum rollers, and only 0.5% off the stock value. The longitudinal odometry constraint is trustworthy; the differential-yaw constraint is not.

Practical consequence: in-place pivot turns and SLAM-critical rotations are disabled in autonomous behaviors, and the state estimator's trust in wheel-derived angular rate was deliberately reduced (its trust in wheel-derived linear speed was not) — the correct response once the drivetrain's true kinematics were understood, not a wheel-hardware defect.

CORE2 serial link

The CORE2 board — FictionLab's official product name is "LeoCore"; this project's software and documentation consistently refer to it as CORE2 — and the onboard Raspberry Pi communicate over a dedicated UART (/dev/serial0, 250 000 baud), decoded by the firmware_message_converter node into standard ROS topics (odometry, joint states, battery, IMU). This link is a known fragility point: sustained checksum-error bursts have been observed and are under active investigation (see the Nav Modes page's data-flow diagram for where this node sits in the pipeline).

Wheels: Standard vs Mecanum

The rover ships with rubber tyres and can be refitted with a mecanum set. The choice is not cosmetic: it changes the effective rolling radius, the way a pivot is physically produced, and therefore how every wheel-derived distance and heading must be read. This section states what is manufacturer specification, what was measured on this particular rover, and where the two disagree.

Current state of this rover, read live from the firmware

Mecanum wheels are physically fitted, but the firmware is running the differential profile: /firmware/mecanum_wheels is false and /etc/ros/setup.bash carries export MECANUM_WHEELS=false. The firmware holds two complete and separate parameter sets — only one of them is in use.

1 · Effective radius: why a mecanum wheel is “smaller” than it looks

A rubber tyre rolls on its outer surface, so its rolling radius is essentially its geometric radius. A mecanum wheel rolls on polyurethane rollers that deform under load, so the distance travelled per revolution corresponds to a radius smaller than the one you would measure with a calliper on the unloaded wheel.

// roller compression, from the two radii above δ = r_geo − r_eff = 64.4 − 62.2 = 2.2 mm δ / r_geo = 2.2 / 64.4 = 3.4 %

2 · What using the wrong radius costs, in metres

Wheel odometry integrates encoder angle into distance through one multiplication. The radius is therefore a pure scale factor on every distance the wheels report: get it wrong by a percentage and every measurement is wrong by that same percentage, in the same direction, forever.

// distance reported by the wheels d̂ = r_used · Σ Δθwheel d_true = r_true · Σ Δθ_wheel // pure scale error, independent of path length ε = (r_used − r_true) / r_true // this rover today: firmware uses stock 0.0625, fitted wheels measure 0.0622 ε = (0.0625 − 0.0622) / 0.0622 = +0.48 % // on the 23.35 m taped-rectangle lap (report §12.16) Δd = 23.35 × 0.0048 = +0.11 m over-reported

Read this honestly: 0.11 m is small next to the 0.449 m loop-closure gap actually measured on that lap, so the radius mismatch is not the dominant error. It is, however, a systematic bias that costs nothing to remove — unlike the rest of the gap, which requires estimator work.

3 · Why a pivot costs 1.76× on standard wheels and 1.0× on mecanum

A four-wheel skid-steer platform has no steering linkage. To rotate, the tyres must slide sideways across the ground — the wheels roll forward while the chassis turns, so the contact patches scrub. That scrub absorbs part of the commanded rotation, which is why the firmware has to over-drive the wheel differential to obtain the yaw it was asked for. A mecanum wheel does not fight this: its rollers are free to turn about their own axes, so the lateral motion a pivot demands is rolling, not sliding.

// ideal differential drive: yaw from the wheel speed difference ωz = r · (ωR − ωL) / b b = wheel_separation // what the firmware actually applies ωz,cmd = k · r · (ωR − ωL) / b k = angular_velocity_multiplier // k = 1.76 is equivalent to an effective yaw track of beff = k · b = 1.76 × 0.358 = 0.630 m i.e. the platform rotates as if its wheels were 63 cm apart, not 35.8 cm — the cost of scrubbing four tyres sideways. // sensitivity of yaw to the two geometric parameters δω/ω = δr/r − δb/b radius and track pull in opposite directions

4 · The third degree of freedom this rover is not using

Because each roller is set at 45° to the wheel axis, the force a mecanum wheel can transmit to the ground is constrained to one diagonal direction. Mounted in the standard X pattern, the four diagonals span the plane: sum them one way and the platform moves forward, another way and it translates sideways without rotating, a third way and it spins on the spot. This is the full three-degree-of-freedom mecanum inverse kinematics, written with this firmware's own mecanum parameters.

// mecanum inverse kinematics, X configuration, rollers at 45° // using /firmware/mecanum_drive as read from this robot r = 0.0635 m l_x = 0.3052/2 = 0.1526 m l_y = 0.370/2 = 0.185 m L = l_x + l_y = 0.3376 m ωFL = ( vx − vy − L·ωz ) / r ωFR = ( vx + vy + L·ωz ) / r ωRL = ( vx + vy − L·ωz ) / r ωRR = ( vx − vy + L·ωz ) / r // pure sideways translation: v_x = 0, ω_z = 0 ωFL = −vy/r ωFR = +vy/r ωRL = +vy/r ωRR = −vy/r two wheels forward, two backward, at equal speed // pure rotation: v_x = v_y = 0 → |ω_i| = L·|ω_z| / r for all four

This rover cannot strafe, and the reason is not the wheels

The hardware is capable of everything above. The configuration is not: with MECANUM_WHEELS=false, the CORE2 firmware runs the two-degree-of-freedom differential model, and the command topic /cmd_vel carries a Twist whose linear.y is simply discarded downstream. There is no vy anywhere in the chain to strafe with. Enabling it is a firmware-side change plus a matching odometry model — a real piece of work, not a checkbox, and it is listed as future work rather than claimed as a capability.

5 · What the mecanum set actually does on this rover

Everything below was measured on this platform during the campaign, not taken from a datasheet.

yaw authority

92–99 %

of commanded yaw actually delivered, 0.15 → 0.40 rad/s, on a healthy battery. Dead zone below ≈0.12 rad/s: the rollers need a breakaway force before anything turns.

battery, not mechanics

10.3 V

An apparent “roller stall” turned out to be motor sag at 10.3 V, not a wheel problem. On this platform, check the battery before theorising about the mechanics.

left/right asymmetry

≈1.65 %

of effective-radius difference between sides, isolated by correlating the gyro against wheel speeds over windows where the commanded wheel speeds were symmetric to within 3 %. Masked in software by a yaw trim of −0.087 rad/s per m/s — a stopgap, not a substitute for measuring each side.

pivot geometry

≈16 cm

of real IMU excursion during a ≈115° turn on the spot: the sensor orbits the pivot centre. This is genuine motion, not estimator error — and it is why visual SLAM is disabled during pure pivots, where landmark depth is unobservable.

// subsystem 03

Electronics & Onboard Sensors

Compute runs Ubuntu 20.04 LTS and ROS Noetic, hosting the CORE2 firmware bridge, the camera driver, and the sensor-sanitizing nodes described below before anything reaches the estimators.

As-verified — Raspberry Pi 4B, not the current default Pi 5

FictionLab's official v1.9 specification lists a Raspberry Pi 5 (Broadcom BCM2712, 2.4 GHz quad-core Cortex-A76, 4 GB LPDDR4X) as the onboard computer. This specific unit, checked directly on the robot (/proc/device-tree/model, free -h), runs a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B instead — Broadcom BCM2711, 1.5 GHz quad-core Cortex-A72, 8 GB LPDDR4 — a confirmed hardware divergence from the current default configuration, not a documentation guess. All ROS Noetic / Ubuntu 20.04 software on this site targets this Pi 4B build.

IMU sanitizing — two guarded failure modes

The onboard IMU (CORE2/LeoCore mainboard, 6-axis, published on /firmware/imu) feeds a dedicated sanitizer node before any estimator sees it. Two independent failure modes were characterized from live data and are guarded in software: single-sample glitches (occasional gyro/accel spikes many orders of magnitude above physical motion, traced to I2C/rosserial framing hiccups) are clamped — any axis exceeding 35 rad/s (gyro) or 160 m/s² (accel) is rejected and replaced by the last good sample — and full sensor freezes (200+ consecutive bit-identical samples, seen after serial desync bursts) trigger an automatic firmware board reset via the /firmware/reset_board service, throttled to one reset per 60 s.

Perception — Intel RealSense D455

Perception's primary sensor is an Intel RealSense D455: a stereo IR pair (infra1/infra2) at 640×480 for visual-inertial tracking, a depth stream for obstacle avoidance, and an internal 6-axis IMU — all delivered over USB3 at up to ~30 Hz.

Feeding the estimators

These sensors feed one of two interchangeable state estimators — OpenVINS (vision + IMU) or MINS (adds wheel odometry, extensible to beacon and LiDAR fixes) — switchable live from the cockpit with no discontinuity in the published pose. The full estimator architecture, live data-flow diagram, and switch mechanism are documented on the Navigation Modes page.
