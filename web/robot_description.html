// subsystem 01

Chassis Architecture & Mobility

The chassis is a 4×4 skid-steer platform, 424 × 445 × 303 mm (L × W × H, excluding camera mast) at ≈7 kg, rated for a 5 kg payload — FictionLab's official v1.9 specification (see the table above). The bounding box measured directly from the CAD model above — 445.8 × 424.6 × 303.4 mm — matches it to within a fraction of a millimeter, confirming the model faithfully represents the as-built unit.

Mass is distributed low and central: the battery pack sits at the geometric center of the chassis floor, beneath the compute and sensor deck, keeping the center of mass close to the wheel-axle plane. This is the standard stability strategy for a skid-steer platform, whose cornering behavior depends on tire-ground friction rather than a steering linkage — a high or off-center mass would directly degrade traction predictability during turns.

The 295 mm wheelbase and 354 mm track width, spread across 4-point ground contact, give the platform a wide, stable footprint for its size, at the cost of the turning-radius flexibility of a wheeled platform with true steering — a trade-off made explicit by the drivetrain findings below.

// subsystem 02

Motorisation & Transmission

Four brushed DC gearmotors (Bühler), each with a quadrature encoder, drive the wheels independently through the CORE2 controller board's motor stage — nominally a differential (skid-steer) drivetrain, commanded as geometry_msgs/Twist and converted to per-wheel setpoints on the robot.

Field discovery — the wheels are Mecanum, not rigid

A field calibration campaign made a consequential discovery: the wheels fitted to this unit are not the plain rigid wheel FictionLab lists in its base specification, but the FictionLab Mecanum wheel set (Leo Addon) — Ø128.8 mm hubs with 45°, free-rolling polyurethane rollers. Commanding a gentle turn produced a large commanded wheel-speed differential with almost no actual chassis rotation (gyroscope and visual heading both near zero): the rollers were absorbing the differential exactly as mecanum kinematics predicts, instead of converting it into yaw.

Straight-line rolling is unaffected: 20 guarded half-second windows over a 2.2 m run gave an effective rolling radius r_eff = 62.2 mm against the 64.4 mm geometric hub radius (IQR ±0.5 mm) — a 3.4% roller-contact compression, typical for mecanum rollers, and only 0.5% off the stock value. The longitudinal odometry constraint is trustworthy; the differential-yaw constraint is not.

Practical consequence: in-place pivot turns and SLAM-critical rotations are disabled in autonomous behaviors, and the state estimator's trust in wheel-derived angular rate was deliberately reduced (its trust in wheel-derived linear speed was not) — the correct response once the drivetrain's true kinematics were understood, not a wheel-hardware defect.

CORE2 serial link

The CORE2 board — FictionLab's official product name is "LeoCore"; this project's software and documentation consistently refer to it as CORE2 — and the onboard Raspberry Pi communicate over a dedicated UART (/dev/serial0, 250 000 baud), decoded by the firmware_message_converter node into standard ROS topics (odometry, joint states, battery, IMU). This link is a known fragility point: sustained checksum-error bursts have been observed and are under active investigation (see the Nav Modes page's data-flow diagram for where this node sits in the pipeline).

// subsystem 03

Electronics & Onboard Sensors

Compute runs Ubuntu 20.04 LTS and ROS Noetic, hosting the CORE2 firmware bridge, the camera driver, and the sensor-sanitizing nodes described below before anything reaches the estimators.

As-verified — Raspberry Pi 4B, not the current default Pi 5

FictionLab's official v1.9 specification lists a Raspberry Pi 5 (Broadcom BCM2712, 2.4 GHz quad-core Cortex-A76, 4 GB LPDDR4X) as the onboard computer. This specific unit, checked directly on the robot (/proc/device-tree/model, free -h), runs a Raspberry Pi 4 Model B instead — Broadcom BCM2711, 1.5 GHz quad-core Cortex-A72, 8 GB LPDDR4 — a confirmed hardware divergence from the current default configuration, not a documentation guess. All ROS Noetic / Ubuntu 20.04 software on this site targets this Pi 4B build.

IMU sanitizing — two guarded failure modes

The onboard IMU (CORE2/LeoCore mainboard, 6-axis, published on /firmware/imu) feeds a dedicated sanitizer node before any estimator sees it. Two independent failure modes were characterized from live data and are guarded in software: single-sample glitches (occasional gyro/accel spikes many orders of magnitude above physical motion, traced to I2C/rosserial framing hiccups) are clamped — any axis exceeding 35 rad/s (gyro) or 160 m/s² (accel) is rejected and replaced by the last good sample — and full sensor freezes (200+ consecutive bit-identical samples, seen after serial desync bursts) trigger an automatic firmware board reset via the /firmware/reset_board service, throttled to one reset per 60 s.

Perception — Intel RealSense D455

Perception's primary sensor is an Intel RealSense D455: a stereo IR pair (infra1/infra2) at 640×480 for visual-inertial tracking, a depth stream for obstacle avoidance, and an internal 6-axis IMU — all delivered over USB3 at up to ~30 Hz.

Feeding the estimators

These sensors feed one of two interchangeable state estimators — OpenVINS (vision + IMU) or MINS (adds wheel odometry, extensible to beacon and LiDAR fixes) — switchable live from the cockpit with no discontinuity in the published pose. The full estimator architecture, live data-flow diagram, and switch mechanism are documented on the Navigation Modes page.
